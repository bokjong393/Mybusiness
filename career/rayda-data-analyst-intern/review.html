What to send

Cover letter draft

Peace Sossa · [phone] · peace.launchoperator@gmail.com

Why it's written that way

  • Line one says what you're applying for. No "I am writing to express my keen interest."
  • French comes early, framed as useful to them — not as a fact about you.
  • The project link is the letter's whole job. Everything else is context for it.
  • "Tell me when I'm wrong" signals coachability, which is what people actually hire interns for.
  • Only claim SQL fluency once it's true. Say the honest version and they'll respect it; claim it and fail a live query, and it's over.
The differentiator

Device Lifecycle Analytics

Anyone can submit a Titanic notebook. Almost nobody submits an analysis of the thing the company sells. Four to six hours.

1

Make the data — 60 min

You don't need real data and shouldn't pretend you have it. Generate about 300 rows in Google Sheets, one per device: device_id, model, country, assigned_date, employee_status, return_date, repair_count, repair_cost_usd, purchase_cost_usd.

Countries: Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Philippines, Brazil. Make some returns late, some never returned, some repaired twice. Realistic messiness is the point.

2

Answer five questions — 2 hrs

These are the questions an IT-ops company loses money on.

  1. Recovery rate — of devices assigned to employees who left, what share came back? By country.
  2. Time-to-deploy — median days from assignment to delivery. Where is it slowest?
  3. Cost per device per year — purchase amortised plus repairs. Which model is actually cheapest once repairs count? Often not the cheapest to buy — that's your headline.
  4. Repair concentration — do 20% of devices generate 80% of repair cost?
  5. Value at risk — total book value of unrecovered devices. One number, in dollars.
3

Five charts — 1 hr

Bar chart for recovery by country. Line for time-to-deploy by month. Nothing 3D, no pie chart with eleven slices. Label the axes.

4

The one-page memo — 1 hr

This is your real differentiator. Three findings, and for each: the number, why it happens, what you'd do about it. An Economics student who writes "recovery in Côte d'Ivoire is 41% against 78% in Nigeria; at $600 a laptop that's $X a year; I'd test a prepaid return label at exit" is thinking like an owner, not a button-pusher. That's who gets hired.

5

Publish, and label it honestly

A Google Sheet set to "anyone with the link can view" is fine; a GitHub repo with a README is better. Put a line at the top: "Synthetic dataset built to model a real device-lifecycle problem." Never imply it's real company data — the honesty costs you nothing and protects you completely.

Want more? Load the same CSV into DB Fiddle or SQLite Online and write the five answers as queries instead of formulas. Screenshot them into the README — now you've demonstrated SQL rather than claimed it.

One week

The SQL sprint

An hour a day. All free, all in-browser, no installation — works on a laptop or a phone.

DayFocus
1SELECT, WHERE, ORDER BY, LIMIT
2GROUP BY, COUNT, SUM, AVG, HAVING
3JOIN — inner and left. The one interviewers test
4CASE WHEN, date functions, NULL handling
5CTEs (WITH … AS) — how real analysts write readable queries
6Window functions: ROW_NUMBER(), RANK(), running totals
7Rewrite your five project questions as SQL queries

SQLBolt · Mode's SQL Tutorial · PostgreSQL Exercises. By day 7 you can honestly say "I write SQL." Not "expert" — nobody believes an intern is.

Interview

What they'll ask

Why data analysis?

Connect it to Economics, not to a trend. "Economics taught me to ask whether a number means what people say it means. Analysis is the same question with better tools."

You have no work experience — why should we take you?

Don't apologise. "I don't have a job history, so I built the thing instead — here's an analysis of device recovery rates and what I'd do about them." Then send the link.

Do you know Segment, PostHog or Customer.io?

"Not yet. I understand what each is for — Segment collects events, PostHog analyses product usage, Customer.io sends lifecycle messages — and I'd expect to be useful on them within a couple of weeks." Confident, honest, specific. Never bluff a tool.

What's a JOIN?

Be ready to say it in one sentence and write one. "It combines rows from two tables on a matching column. A LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the left table even when there's no match — I use it when I don't want to silently drop records."

How would you handle messy data?

Give a process, not an adjective: check duplicates, check nulls, check the date range makes sense, check totals against a known source — then ask whoever owns the system about anything that still looks wrong. That last step is the one juniors forget and managers love.

Your two questions for them

"What would a successful first three months look like for this intern?" and "Is there an existing data person I'd be learning from, or am I the first?" The second answer changes the job enormously — ask it.
