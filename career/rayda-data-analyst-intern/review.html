On the money. Nigeria's national minimum wage is ₦70,000/month, so this lands at roughly 1.1× to 1.4× minimum wage for a full-time technology role. That's low, and it's better to know it's low now than to find out later.

Two things push the real value up. Remote means no transport, no lodging, no Lagos rent — against an office job paying the same, that gap is real money every month. And what you're actually buying is the first line on your CV, a named analyst to learn from, and a year of provable remote work with a funded company. At 21 and 200L with no work history, that's worth more than the gap between ₦100k and ₦150k. It stops being worth more once you have it — so treat this as a twelve-month trade, not a career.

On the hours — this is the real decision

Full time alongside 200L Economics isn't a formality. The only reason it's feasible at all is that Miva is asynchronous: you control when you study. But full time is full time, and 200L into 300L is when Economics gets heavier.

  • "I can do 40 hours and still pass well."Apply, and take it if offered.
  • "I can do maybe 20–25 hours."Still apply — and ask about hours in the first conversation, not after an offer. A 27-person startup flexes on this constantly, especially for a paid intern. Asking is normal; asking late is what looks bad.
  • "My grades would suffer."Then don't. The degree is the asset you're already two years into.

Don't resolve this by staying silent and hoping. Ask them: "The posting lists this as full time — is there flexibility on hours for a current student, or is 40 hours firm?" A good company answers plainly. The answer tells you a lot about them either way.

The differentiator

Asset Lifecycle Analytics

A small version of Rayda's own business, in their own words — acquire, manage, track and dispose. Building it in Looker Studio closes a stated "advantage" requirement at the same time. Two birds, one afternoon.

1

Make the data — 60 min

You don't need real data and shouldn't pretend you have it. Generate about 300 rows in Google Sheets, one per device: device_id, model, country, purchase_cost_usd, assigned_date, delivered_date, employee_status, return_date, repair_count, repair_cost_usd, disposal_status, resale_value_usd.

Countries: Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Côte d'Ivoire, Philippines, Brazil. Some returns late, some never returned, some repaired twice, some sold on and some scrapped. Realistic messiness is the point — cleaning it is literally duty number one in the posting.

2

Answer six questions — 2 hrs

These are the questions an asset lifecycle company loses money on. The last one exists because dispose is in their own description of themselves.

  1. Recovery rate — of devices assigned to employees who left, what share came back? By country.
  2. Time-to-deploy — median days from assignment to delivery. Where is it slowest?
  3. True cost per device per year — purchase amortised plus repairs. Which model is actually cheapest once repairs count? Often not the cheapest to buy — that's your headline.
  4. Repair concentration — do 20% of devices generate 80% of repair cost?
  5. Value at risk — total book value of unrecovered devices. One number, in dollars.
  6. Recovery at disposal — what share of purchase cost comes back as resale value, and how does it vary by model and by age at retirement?
3

Build it in Looker Studio — 1 hr

Connect the Google Sheet directly. Free, browser-based, nothing to install, and one of the three tools the posting names. Six tiles: scorecards for the headline numbers, a bar chart for recovery by country, a time series for time-to-deploy. Nothing 3D, no pie chart with eleven slices. Label the axes, then set sharing to "anyone with the link can view".

4

The one-page memo — 1 hr

This is your real differentiator. Three findings, and for each: the number, why it happens, what you'd do about it. An Economics student who writes "recovery in Côte d'Ivoire is 41% against 78% in Nigeria; at $600 a laptop that's $X a year; I'd test a prepaid return label issued at exit" is thinking like an owner, not a button-pusher. It also maps straight onto the posting's "translate business questions into simple data analyses and insights."

5

Label it honestly

Put one line at the top of the dashboard and the memo: "Synthetic dataset built to model a real asset-lifecycle problem." Never imply it's real company data.

For the extra point: load the same CSV into DB Fiddle or SQLite Online and write two or three answers as queries. Screenshot them into the memo — now "basic knowledge of SQL" is demonstrated rather than claimed, which is exactly the level they asked for.

The one gate

Google Sheets

"Familiarity with Excel or Google Sheets" is the only tool requirement not hedged with "is an advantage". These should be automatic before an interview:

  • Pivot tablesGroup by country, sum a cost column. Build three from your device data.
  • XLOOKUPPull a value from another table by matching an ID. VLOOKUP if that's what you know.
  • SUMIFSConditional totals — the workhorse of every business report. COUNTIFS too.
  • IFFlag rows: returned late, never returned, over-repaired.
  • Date mathsdelivered_date − assigned_date, then a median of it.
  • ChartsBar, line, and knowing which to use when.
  • Clean-upRemove duplicates, TRIM, split columns, spot blanks.

Building the project exercises every one of these. That's deliberate — don't study them separately, just build the thing.

Interview

What they'll ask

Why data analysis?

Connect it to Economics, not to a trend. "Economics taught me to ask whether a number means what people say it means. Analysis is the same question with better tools."

You have no work experience — why should we take you?

Don't apologise. "I don't have a job history, so I built the thing instead — here's an asset lifecycle dashboard and what I'd do about what it shows." Then send the link.

How comfortable are you with SQL?

They asked for basic, so answer at that level without shrinking. "Basic — I can select, filter, group and join, and I've written queries against my own dataset. I'd expect to be faster within a few weeks on real tables." Never bluff a level you can't demonstrate live.

Do you know Power BI or Tableau?

"I built my project dashboard in Looker Studio. The concepts carry — data source, dimensions, measures, filters — so I'd expect to pick up Power BI quickly if that's what the team uses."

How would you handle messy data?

Give a process, not an adjective: check duplicates, check nulls, check the date range makes sense, check totals against a known source — then ask whoever owns the system about anything that still looks wrong. That last step is the one juniors forget and managers love, and it's duty five in the posting.

Can you work full time?

Have your real answer ready before they ask. If you can, say so plainly. If you need flex, say it early and without apology: "I'm a 200-level student at an online university, so my schedule is genuinely flexible — but I want to be straight with you about it. Is there room to agree hours, or is 40 a week firm?"

Your two questions for them

"What would a successful first three months look like for this intern?" and "I saw the role supports an existing Data Analyst — what does that working relationship look like day to day?" The second shows you read the posting properly. Very few candidates do.
