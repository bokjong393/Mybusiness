# Interview Prep — Finance Intern

## 1. The five questions you will almost certainly get

**"Tell me about yourself."**
Ninety seconds, three beats: *present* (second-year Economics student at Miva, bilingual English and
French) → *proof* (one concrete thing you have built or handled — a model, a reconciliation, a project)
→ *why here* (what draws you to this role and this company). Rehearse it out loud until it is smooth.
Never recite your CV chronologically.

**"Why finance?"**
Have a real answer, not "I like numbers." A strong version for you: *"Economics taught me why prices and
demand move. Finance is where that gets applied to decisions with money behind them — and I want to be
on the side that decides, not just describes."* Then attach it to your ambition honestly: you want to
understand how businesses actually make and keep money, because you intend to run one.

**"Why should we take you when you're only in your second year?"**
Do not get defensive. *"You'd get someone who has time now, learns fast, and isn't juggling final-year
projects. I'm available [[N]] hours a week, my course is fully online so my schedule is flexible, and I
work in French as well as English."* Confidence, then facts.

**"What are your weaknesses?"**
Pick something real, small and fixable, and show the fix in motion. *"My exposure to accounting software
is limited — I've worked in Excel. I'm working through [[QuickBooks/Wave]] now so I can be useful on
day one."* Never "I'm a perfectionist."

**"Where do you see yourself in five years?"**
Truthful and employer-safe: *"Working in finance with real analytical depth — and eventually running
something of my own, which is exactly why I want to learn how a finance function actually operates from
the inside."* Ambition to build your own business is an asset here; it reads as drive. Just do not
frame the internship as a stepping stone you plan to abandon.

## 2. The technical questions — know these cold

An intern is not expected to value a company. You *are* expected to know these five things.

**"Walk me through the three financial statements."**
- **Income statement (P&L):** revenue minus costs over a period → profit. It shows performance.
- **Balance sheet:** assets = liabilities + equity, at a single point in time. It shows position.
- **Cash flow statement:** actual cash in and out over a period, split into operating, investing and
  financing. It shows survival.

**"What's the difference between profit and cash flow?"**
The one every intern gets wrong, and the one that matters most in a Nigerian SME context.
*Profit is an accounting opinion; cash is a fact.* You book revenue when you make the sale, but if the
customer pays in 60 days, the cash is not there yet. A profitable business absolutely can — and often
does — go under because it cannot pay salaries this month. Have a one-line example ready.

**"What is working capital?"**
Current assets minus current liabilities — the money tied up in running the business day to day
(inventory and receivables, less what you owe suppliers). Too little and you cannot pay your bills; too
much and your cash is sitting idle in stock.

**"What is a bank reconciliation and why does it matter?"**
Matching your own records against the bank statement, then explaining every difference — uncleared
cheques, bank charges, timing gaps, and errors or fraud. It matters because it is the control that
catches money going missing. This is real intern work: expect to be asked whether you have done one.

**"What is depreciation and where does it appear?"**
Spreading an asset's cost over its useful life. It is an expense on the P&L, it reduces the asset's
value on the balance sheet, and it is added back on the cash flow statement — because no cash actually
left the business.

> **Nigeria-specific bonus, and it will impress:** be able to talk for thirty seconds about how naira
> depreciation and inflation hit a company's numbers — imported input costs rise, FX losses appear on
> the P&L, and prior-year comparisons stop meaning much in real terms. Very few interns can do this;
> as an Economics student you should. Read one recent annual report before the interview and you will
> have a live example to point to.

## 3. Excel — expect a practical test

Be genuinely able to do these. If you cannot yet, one focused weekend fixes it.

- `XLOOKUP` / `VLOOKUP` — pull a value from another table
- `SUMIFS` / `COUNTIFS` — total by category and date
- **Pivot tables** — summarise a transaction list by month and category
- `IF`, nested conditions, `IFERROR`
- Absolute vs. relative references (`$A$1` vs `A1`) — a classic screening question
- Basic charts, and clean number formatting (thousands separators, currency, no 8-decimal noise)
- Keyboard: `Ctrl+Shift+↓` to select a column, `Alt+=` to autosum, `F4` to lock a reference

**Practice properly:** rebuild a real dataset. Download three months of any bank statement or NBS price
data, clean it, categorise it, and build a pivot summary with a chart. That is the actual test.

## 4. Questions you ask them

Always have three. Not asking any is read as not caring.

1. "What would I actually be working on in the first month?"
2. "What does a great intern look like in this role — what separates the ones you'd hire back?"
3. "Who would I be reporting to, and how does the team review work?"
4. *(If remote)* "How does the team stay in sync day to day — what tools and what hours?"

Leave salary and stipend to them for the first conversation. If they ask what you expect, it is fair to
say: "I'd like to understand the role fully first — what range have you budgeted for the internship?"

## 5. Logistics

- **Test the tech an hour before** a video interview: camera, microphone, internet, backup mobile data.
  Have their phone number ready in case the call drops — and if it does, call back immediately and
  calmly. It happens; how you handle it is the actual test.
- **Plain background, good light in front of you** (a window facing you, not behind you).
- **Dress one level above** what you think they wear.
- **Have on the desk:** printed CV, the job posting, your three questions, pen and paper. Taking a note
  when they explain something reads as seriousness.
- **Send a thank-you email within 24 hours.** Four sentences: thank them, name one specific thing from
  the conversation, restate your interest, sign off. Most candidates skip it.

## 6. If the interview is in French

Given your background this is a real possibility with francophone-facing employers — and a real
opportunity. Prepare the core vocabulary so you are not translating live:

| English | French |
|---|---|
| Income statement | le compte de résultat |
| Balance sheet | le bilan |
| Cash flow | la trésorerie / le flux de trésorerie |
| Revenue | le chiffre d'affaires |
| Bank reconciliation | le rapprochement bancaire |
| Accounts receivable | les créances clients |
| Accounts payable | les dettes fournisseurs |
| Working capital | le besoin en fonds de roulement (BFR) |
| Budget variance | l'écart budgétaire |
| Internship / intern | le stage / le stagiaire |

Say early that you work in both languages and let them choose. Offering the choice is itself the
impressive move.

## 7. Your specific talking points

Generic prep above; this is yours. Have each of these ready as a 30–60 second answer.

**The reconciliation story.** They are hiring for reconciliation. You do it. Be ready to describe the
ice-block operation concretely: what comes in, how you match cash against sales, what you do when it
does not balance, what goes into the Director's monthly report. Have a real example of a discrepancy
you found and how you chased it down. This is your strongest single answer — do not let modesty about
the scale flatten it. Small business reconciliation is *harder* in some ways: no system does it for you.

**"Sixteen consecutive cycles."** When they ask about reliability or deadlines, this is the answer. A
number beats an adjective every time.

**The mobile money study.** Be able to explain in plain language what you found — that a third-place
rank by account count hid the region's lowest per-capita penetration — and *why the distinction matters*
to someone making a decision. This demonstrates exactly the skill a finance team wants: not reporting
the number, but noticing what it conceals.

**The limitations section.** If asked about attention to detail or integrity, do not say "I'm detail
oriented". Say: you published a limitations section distinguishing registered from active accounts,
because secondary coverage routinely conflates the two. That is integrity demonstrated rather than
claimed, and it is the kind of answer interviewers remember.

**Why real estate, given your research is in financial access?** Have this ready — it is the obvious
challenge. Honest answer: your current research is on digital real estate, and rent-to-own sits exactly
where household financial access and property meet. That is not a pivot; it is the same question.

**The gap they may probe: Abomey-Calavi.** If asked why you did not complete the Licence, answer briefly
and without apology, then move to what you took from it — the mathematics, programming and SQL you still
use. Do not over-explain. A short, unembarrassed answer closes the topic; a long one opens it.

**Relocation.** Expect it early, possibly as the first question. Have a definite answer: when you can be
in Lagos, that you have thought about accommodation, that you are not going to accept and then wobble.
Vagueness here undoes everything else.

**The one you should ask them.** "What does the reconciliation process look like today — how are
instalment payments matched to buyer accounts?" It signals you know where the difficulty lives, and
their answer tells you whether the job is real analytical work or data entry.
